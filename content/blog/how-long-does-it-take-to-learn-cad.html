"How long will it take me to learn SolidWorks?" Trainees ask this before almost anything else, and the internet's answers range from "a weekend" to "years". Both are true, depending on what "learn" means. Here's a realistic timeline based on the courses I actually teach.

The course hours

These are the full guided courses, from first launch to advanced topics:

  • SolidWorks: about 54 hours (17 sessions)
  • Autodesk Inventor: about 58 hours (18 sessions)
  • Fusion 360: 60–76 hours (21 sessions, including CAM, simulation and generative design)
  • Creo Parametric: about 44 hours (14 sessions)
  • Autodesk Revit: about 52 hours (16 sessions)
  • SolidWorks Simulation (FEA): about 24 hours, once you know SolidWorks
  • SolidWorks Flow Simulation (CFD): about 14 hours, once you know SolidWorks

At two sessions a week, a full mechanical CAD course takes roughly 2 to 3 months (SolidWorks' 17 sessions take about 9 weeks). With more sessions a week, it can be done in a few weeks.

Guided hours are only half of it

The single biggest factor is practice between sessions. A rough rule that holds up well: for every hour of training, spend at least one hour practising on your own. Trainees who do this finish faster and remember far more than those who only practise in class. Practice doesn't need to be fancy: model objects around you, like a phone stand, a door hinge or a bottle.

What you can do, and when

After about 2 weeks

You can sketch with proper relations, build simple parts (brackets, plates, shafts) and make a basic drawing. Useful for school projects and simple workshop parts.

After about 2 months

You can build multi-part assemblies with correct mates, produce manufacturing drawings with a bill of materials, and design sheet-metal parts with flat patterns for laser cutting. This is the level most small fabrication jobs need, and where people start doing paid work.

After about 6 months of regular use

You're fast. You model with design intent so changes take minutes, you reach for surfaces, weldments or simulation when a job needs them, and you're ready for a certification such as the CSWA (Certified SOLIDWORKS Associate).

What speeds it up

  • Learn sketching properly first. Fully defined sketches make everything after them easier. Skipping this is the most common reason people stall.
  • Work on a real project. Designing one complete thing, such as the bench vise my SolidWorks trainees build, teaches more than a hundred disconnected exercises.
  • Use shortcuts from day one. In AutoCAD especially, command aliases make a huge difference: see 10 AutoCAD shortcuts that save hours.
  • Pick one program and go deep before adding a second. The skills transfer: after one parametric CAD program, the next takes a fraction of the time.

Which one should you start with?

That depends on your goals and the industry you want to work in. I compare the three most popular options in SolidWorks vs Inventor vs Fusion 360. For building design, Revit is the one to learn; for 2D drafting, AutoCAD.
