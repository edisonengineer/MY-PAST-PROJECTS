Designing for Manufacturability:

5 CAD Tips to Reduce Machining Costs

When transitioning a mechanical concept from a 3D CAD model to a physical prototype, one of the most common friction points is manufacturing cost. A design that looks flawless in SolidWorks or Autodesk Inventor can become surprisingly expensive if it doesn't account for how CNC milling machines, lathes, or laser cutters actually remove material.

By applying core Design for Manufacturability (DFM) principles during the modeling stage, you can optimize parts for faster production, reduced tool wear, and significantly lower costs.

Match Internal Corner Radii to Standard End Mill Sizes

Sharp internal vertical corners cannot be produced by round, rotating CNC drill bits. Always add fillets to internal vertical edges. To keep tool changes minimal:

  • Make the corner radius slightly larger than the standard milling tool radius (e.g., use a 3.2 mm radius for a 6 mm end mill). This allows the tool to turn smoothly without grinding into the corner.
  • Avoid Overly Deep and Narrow Pockets. Deep cavities require long, slender cutting tools that are prone to deflection, chatter, and tool breakage:
  • Limit pocket depth to no more than 3 to 4 times the tool diameter.
  • If a deep pocket is necessary, consider breaking the design into a modular two-piece assembly.

Standardize Hole Sizes and Thread Depths

Custom hole dimensions require specialty drill bits, increasing setup time and tooling expenses:

  • Use standard tap drill sizes (e.g., M3, M4, M6, or M8) available in standard tool carousels.
  • Thread holes only as deep as necessary—typically 2 to 3 times the fastener diameter provides maximum holding strength.

Keep Wall Thicknesses Consistent and Realistic

Thin walls vibrate under high cutting forces, leading to poor surface finishes and dimensional inaccuracy:

  • Maintain a minimum wall thickness of at least 0.8 mm for metals (aluminum/steel) and 1.5 mm for plastics.
  • Ensure uniform wall thickness across the part to prevent thermal warping during machining or heat treatment.

Design Parts with a Single Setup in Mind

Every time a machinist has to reposition or flip a stock piece in a vise, labor costs increase:

  • Try to keep all critical features (holes, slots, pockets) aligned along a single primary axis.
  • If multi-axis machining is required, ensure the part has flat, parallel reference surfaces for secure clamping.

Final Takeaway

Designing for manufacturability doesn't mean sacrificing performance or aesthetic appeal. Integrating these practical guidelines early in your CAD workflow ensures your prototypes are not only functionally sound, but also cost-effective and ready for seamless production.
