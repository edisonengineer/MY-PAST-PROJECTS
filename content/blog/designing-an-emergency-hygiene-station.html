After a natural disaster, clean water and sanitation quickly become the most urgent needs for displaced families. That was the starting point for the Autodesk Designathon 2026: design a compact, rapidly deployable and sustainable hygiene station where people can wash their hands, get filtered water and keep up basic hygiene in difficult conditions.

The brief, in short

The design had to fit inside a 2 m × 2 m area and include:

  • Water storage built around standard 19-litre carboys or 2.5-litre bottles, the containers aid teams can actually find after a disaster
  • A dedicated filtration area (shown conceptually; it didn't need to work physically)
  • A storage area for wastewater
  • A washing station where at least two people can wash their hands at the same time
  • Realistic assembly details, like fasteners and fillets, and real materials from the Fusion library

Start with the path of the water

Instead of starting with the outer shape, I followed a drop of water from the moment it goes in to the moment it leaves. That gave the station four clear layers, stacked from top to bottom, so gravity does most of the work.

1. Storage on top

Nine inverted 19-litre carboys sit on a header tank at the top. Together they hold about 170 litres, enough for many handwashes before anyone needs to refill. Because the carboys plug straight in, a full one can be swapped for an empty one in seconds.

2. Filtration you can see

From the header tank, water passes down through three filter columns in an enclosed chamber. I used clear acrylic panels around it so that users can see the water is being treated, and maintenance teams can check the filters without taking anything apart.

3. Two people can wash at once

Filtered water feeds two taps over stainless-steel basins on opposite sides of the frame. Putting them on opposite sides, instead of next to each other, means two people can wash at the same time without crowding each other, which matters when there's a queue.

4. Wastewater kept apart

Used water leaves each basin through a U-bend and flows into a separate wastewater tank at the base, with a drain valve to empty it. Keeping dirty water physically below and apart from the clean supply was one of the most important decisions in the design.

The frame

Everything sits on a cross-braced steel frame with aluminium columns. The bracing keeps the station rigid while it carries the weight of the full carboys. I modelled the fasteners and edge fillets as the brief asked, because those details are what make a model look like something that could actually be built.

Fusion 360 tips from this project

  • Build in components from the start. Each part (tank, filters, basins, frame) was its own component, which made joints and changes much easier.
  • Use section analysis. Cutting through the model is the quickest way to check that the internal parts line up.
  • Use transparent materials on purpose. Clear acrylic and glass let the renders show the internal layout without exploded views.
  • Render several angles. A perspective view sells the idea, while front and top views prove the dimensions.

What I'd improve next

If I took this design further, I'd look at a frame that folds flat for transport, a simple water-level window on the header tank, and a foot-pedal tap so people don't have to touch the tap with dirty hands.
