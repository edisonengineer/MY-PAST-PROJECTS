"How much for a 3D model?" is a bit like asking "How much for a house?" The honest answer is: it depends. A clear brief is the fastest way to get an accurate quote and a result that's right first time. Here's what to include.

1. What is it, and what does it do?

Describe the part or product in one or two sentences, and explain its job. "A bracket that holds a 5 kg motor to a steel frame" tells a designer far more than "a bracket".

2. What do you already have?

Share everything, even if it's rough:

  • Hand sketches with dimensions, even on paper
  • Photos of an existing part, ideally next to a ruler
  • Old CAD files or PDF drawings
  • A physical sample that can be measured

The more you provide, the less time is spent guessing, and the lower your cost.

3. Which files do you need back?

Different uses need different formats:

  • STEP or IGES to share 3D models with other CAD software or suppliers
  • STL for 3D printing
  • DWG / DXF for laser cutting and 2D workflows
  • PDF drawings for the workshop
  • Native files (SolidWorks, Inventor, Fusion 360) if you'll edit the model yourself

4. Material and manufacturing method

If you know how it will be made (laser-cut sheet metal, machined aluminium, 3D-printed plastic), say so. It affects how the part should be designed. If you don't know yet, that's fine: say that too and ask for advice.

5. Loads and conditions (for simulation)

If you need a strength or thermal check, describe the forces, pressures or temperatures involved and how the part is held. Even rough estimates help to set up a meaningful analysis.

6. Deadline and budget

Be upfront about when you need it and roughly what you can spend. A designer can then suggest the best approach within your limits. A simpler model now and refinements later, for example.
