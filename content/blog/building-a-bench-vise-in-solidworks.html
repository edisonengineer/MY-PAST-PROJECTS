After weeks of learning tools one at a time, trainees need one project that brings everything together: clean sketches, solid features, editing, sheet metal, and an assembly that actually moves. For my SolidWorks course, that project is a small parallel-guide bench vise.

The brief

A lead screw drives a sliding jaw along two guide rods. The jaws are 100 mm wide and open up to 80 mm. The blocks and screw are solid parts; the base plate and name plate are sheet metal. Everything is in millimetres, and every sketch must be fully defined: no blue lines allowed.

Ten parts, each teaching something

  • Fixed jaw block: extrusions, through holes and Hole Wizard tapped holes (M8 and M5)
  • Sliding jaw block: made with Save As from the fixed block and then edited. This is the editing practice: change dimensions, swap holes, add a pin hole
  • Rear bearing block: an M14 tapped hole for the lead screw
  • Jaw plates: serrations from one V-groove and a 15-groove linear pattern, with countersunk M5 holes
  • Guide rods, lead screw, handle and retaining pin: revolved parts, a cosmetic M14 × 2.0 thread and a groove for the pin
  • Base plate (3 mm sheet metal): base flange, edge flanges for stiffness, slotted mounting holes and a flat pattern
  • Name plate (1 mm sheet metal): rounded corners, holes and optional extruded text

Trainees each model a share of the parts to a written specification, then everyone assembles the full vise from the agreed class files. That's exactly how design teams work.

The assembly: where it comes alive

The base plate is fixed, the blocks are mated to it, and the guide rods are made concentric with their holes. Then two details turn a static model into a mechanism:

  • A distance mate with limits (0 to 80 mm) between the jaw faces, so the vise can't open further than the real one would
  • A screw mate at 2.0 mm per revolution, so turning the handle moves the jaw, just like the real thread

Trainees finish with an exploded view, a section through the screw axis, and an interference check that must come back clean.

Checking the work

A model that looks right isn't always right. So the brief gives check values everyone can compare against:

  • Closed length: about 253 mm; fully open: about 333 mm
  • Jaw opening at the limit: 80.0 mm
  • Screw advance per handle turn: 2.0 mm
  • No interference anywhere in the assembly

The design change

Finally, the real test of good modelling: trainees put the jaw width and rod spacing into global variables, then change the jaw width from 100 mm to 120 mm. The blocks, plates, base and assembly must all rebuild without errors. If a model survives that, it was built with proper design intent.
