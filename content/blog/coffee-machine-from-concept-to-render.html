Between May and June 2026 I designed a compact countertop espresso machine in Fusion 360, from the first rough layout to finished photorealistic renders. Here's how the design came together, and a few tips you can use on your own product designs.

Start with what the machine has to do

Before drawing anything, I listed the jobs the machine has to do: store water, filter it, brew the coffee and catch the drips. Each job became a zone of the machine, and the overall shape came from arranging those zones as compactly as possible.

Water on top

Two inverted bottles sit on top of the machine and feed water down into it. Putting the water at the top keeps the footprint small, and it makes refilling as easy as swapping a bottle.

Show the inside

One side of the machine is a clear acrylic panel. It shows the twin filter cartridges and the internal layout, so you can see at a glance when the filters need attention. It also turns the engineering into part of the look.

The brewing area

At the front, the group head takes a portafilter with a wooden handle, over a perforated stainless-steel drip tray. The tray is sized to catch spills from a full cup and is easy to pull out and rinse.

Choosing materials

A product's materials do as much for its look as its shape. I used a small, consistent palette:

  • Dark bamboo trim for warmth
  • Anodised aluminium for the main body
  • Stainless steel where water and heat are involved
  • ABS plastic for moulded parts
  • Clear acrylic for the side panel

Rendering tips in Fusion 360

  • Apply real appearances early. Seeing materials while you model helps you make better design decisions, not just better pictures.
  • Pick an environment that matches the product. A real-world backdrop and ground shadows make a product feel solid.
  • Render a section view. A cut-through render explains the inside of a product better than any amount of text.
  • Keep the camera at eye level for hero shots. It shows the product the way people will actually see it on a counter.

What I learned

Designing around functions first, and only then the shape, made every later decision easier. When I wasn't sure where a part should go, I went back to the job it had to do.
