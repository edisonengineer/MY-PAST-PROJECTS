"Can you teach me SolidWorks?" is the question I hear most from engineering students and young technicians in Nairobi. The honest follow-up is: which SolidWorks? The software is huge. Most people need a clear path from a blank screen to a part a workshop can actually make, without getting lost in menus they'll never use.

Here's exactly what my SolidWorks course covers, in the order we cover it, and why each step matters.

The shape of the course

The full course is 17 sessions, about 54 hours. Sessions are 2 or 4 hours, one-on-one or in a small group, online or in person in Nairobi. Every session mixes a short demonstration with hands-on practice on real parts, and you keep all the practice files.

1. Foundations (sessions 1–3)

We start with the things that make everything else faster: setting up SolidWorks properly, managing files, and mouse and keyboard habits. Then comes the most important skill in parametric CAD: sketching. You'll learn sketch tools, relations and dimensions until every sketch is fully defined. A fully defined sketch is the difference between a model you can edit in seconds and one that falls apart when you change a number.

2. Solid modelling and editing (sessions 4–6)

From sketches to solid parts: extrude, revolve, sweep, loft and boundary features, reference geometry, and removing material. Then the editing tools you'll use daily: fillets, chamfers, linear, circular and curve-driven patterns, mirror, shell and draft. We finish with advanced shapes such as domes, indents and flex for the parts that aren't just boxes and cylinders.

3. Assemblies and motion (sessions 7–8)

Real products are assemblies. You'll insert components, mate them correctly (standard and advanced mates), work both bottom-up and top-down, make exploded views and a bill of materials, and run a basic motion study. We also use the Toolbox library and smart fasteners, so you never model a standard bolt by hand again.

4. Surfaces and drawings (sessions 9–10)

Surfacing for curved, consumer-style shapes, then manufacturing drawings: choosing sheets, adding views, dimensions and annotations, an exploded assembly view, and your own title block. A drawing is what a fabricator actually works from, so we treat it as a deliverable, not an afterthought.

5. Making things (sessions 11–16)

This is where SolidWorks pays for itself in a Kenyan workshop:

  • Quick checks: SimulationXpress for a first stress check, FloXpress for flow, DFMXpress for manufacturability, plus costing
  • Mould tools: parting lines, shut-off and parting surfaces, tooling split and core
  • Sheet metal: base flanges, bends, bend allowance and K-factor, and flat patterns ready for laser cutting
  • Weldments: structural members, end caps, weld beads and weld symbols on drawings
  • 3D printing and Model Based Definition
  • An introduction to SolidCAM for CNC milling toolpaths

6. Presenting your work (session 17)

Rendering: appearances, scenes and decals, so your portfolio and client presentations look professional.

The project that ties it together

Learning tools one by one is not the same as designing something. So the course ends with a real mechanism: a parallel-guide bench vise with a lead screw, guide rods, serrated jaws and a sheet-metal base plate. Each trainee models a share of the parts to a written specification, then everyone assembles the full vise, adds a screw mate so the jaw actually moves, and produces an A3 drawing set. You can spin the finished vise in 3D on its project page.

Who it's for

  • Engineering students who want more than the CAD hours their timetable allows, or are preparing for the CSWA certification
  • Technicians and fabricators who want to move from hand sketches to proper drawings and flat patterns
  • Company teams standardising on SolidWorks

One-on-one courses follow the same order, but we can skip what you already know or spend longer where you need it.
