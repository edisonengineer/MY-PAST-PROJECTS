A design can look perfect on screen and still be a nightmare to make. Design for Manufacturing (DFM) is the habit of designing parts with the workshop in mind from the very start, so they're quicker, cheaper and more reliable to produce.

During my industrial attachment at KIRDI, I designed and fabricated jiko stove components from sheet metal. Applying DFM principles there made the difference between parts that assembled cleanly and parts that needed rework. Here are the ideas that matter most.

Why DFM matters

Most of a product's manufacturing cost is decided at the design stage, by the choice of material, the number of parts, the tolerances and the processes they require. Changing a CAD model is cheap. Changing a production run is not.

1. Reduce the number of parts

Every extra part needs to be made, stored, handled and assembled. Ask whether two parts could become one, for example a bent sheet-metal bracket instead of two plates welded together. Fewer parts usually means fewer fasteners, fewer tolerances to stack up and faster assembly.

2. Design for the process you'll actually use

A part made by laser cutting and bending has different rules from a part machined on a lathe or a mill. For sheet metal in particular:

  • Bend radii: very tight bends can crack the material. Choosing an inside bend radius at least equal to the sheet thickness is a common starting point.
  • Holes near bends: holes and slots placed too close to a bend line can distort when the part is bent. Keep them a sensible distance away.
  • Small holes: holes much smaller than the sheet thickness can be hard to cut cleanly.

These are rules of thumb. Every workshop's machines and materials are different, so always confirm the numbers with your fabricator before you finalise a design.

3. Only tighten tolerances where they matter

Tight tolerances cost money: slower machining, more inspection and more rejected parts. Apply them only to features that really need them, such as bearing seats and mating surfaces, and use standard, looser tolerances everywhere else.

4. Use standard materials and parts

Design around stock sheet thicknesses, bar sizes and off-the-shelf fasteners, bearings and seals. Standard items are cheaper, easier to find and easier to replace later.

5. Think about assembly and access

Can a person actually reach that bolt with a spanner? Can a part only fit one way, so it can't be assembled wrongly? Features like self-locating tabs and slots in sheet metal make assembly faster and more accurate.

6. Talk to the workshop early

The best DFM tool is a conversation. Machinists and fabricators spot problems in minutes that might take weeks to discover on the shop floor. Share your drawings early and ask what would make the part easier to produce.

DFM in your CAD workflow

Modern CAD tools help. Sheet-metal tools in SolidWorks, Inventor and Fusion 360 build bend allowances into flat patterns automatically, and simulation lets you check strength before cutting any metal. My final-year project takes this further, using simulation to predict when sheet metal will fail during forming, before a single part is made.
