Most welders price a gate the same way: a quick look, a number from experience, and a hope that the steel comes out right. Sometimes it does. But when the customer chose thicker tube, or the bars are closer together, or the posts have to go deep, that "experience price" quietly eats your profit. Here is a simple method that works for any gate, grill or railing, with a full worked example.

1. Write the cut list first

Every price starts with the pieces. For a two-leaf swing gate that means, for each leaf: two frame uprights, a top and bottom rail cut to fit between them, the middle rails, and the bars. Then the two posts, including the part that goes into the ground (usually 600 mm or more).

Count the bars properly. If the customer wants gaps of at most 100 mm, a leaf with 1,695 mm of space inside the frame needs 13 bars of 25 mm tube, not 12. One bar short per bay on a big gate is easy to miss and it is steel you pay for.

2. Turn the cut list into lengths to buy

You don't buy metres, you buy 6 m lengths. Fit the longest pieces first, then fill the gaps with shorter ones, and leave about 3 mm for each saw cut. A 2,100 mm upright fits twice in a length with 1,800 mm left over, enough for one 1,695 mm rail but not two. Those offcuts are why "metres used × price per metre" always comes out too low. Expect 10–20% waste on a typical gate.

3. Add what people forget

  • Welding rods, grinding and cutting discs, gas: about 6–10% of the steel cost.
  • Primer and paint: work out the outside area of every piece (a 25 mm square tube has 0.1 m² per metre) plus both sides of any sheet.
  • Fittings: hinges (three per leaf on a tall gate), drop bolts, lock and handle. On a sliding gate: wheels, guide rollers and the track.
  • Fitting on site and transport.

4. Charge labour by the kilo

Labour grows with the amount of steel you cut, weld and grind. Charging per kg of steel (many workshops use KES 70–120 per kg) keeps big gates and small grills fair, and it stops you under-pricing a heavy gate because "it's just one job".

5. Worked example: a 3.6 m two-leaf gate

A gate 3,600 mm wide and 2,100 mm high, frame in 50 × 50 × 2 tube, one middle rail in 38 × 38, bars in 25 × 25 at no more than 100 mm gaps, two 100 × 100 posts with 600 mm in the ground, painted and fitted. Using example prices of about KES 170 per kg of steel:

ItemAmount
Steel: 14 lengths (3 × 50 mm, 1 × 38 mm, 9 × 25 mm, 1 × 100 mm)KES 30,450
Rods, discs & gas (8%)KES 2,436
Primer & paint: 10.9 m²KES 2,723
Labour: 159 kg at KES 90/kgKES 14,330
Fittings: 6 hinges, 2 drop bolts, lock & handleKES 4,400
Fitting on siteKES 1,500
Your costKES 55,839
Profit (25%)KES 13,960
Price to the customerKES 69,798

Notice how much the steel is: more than half the cost. If you had quoted from "metres used" instead of lengths bought, you would have been short by thousands of shillings before you even started welding.

6. Send a proper quote

A written quote with the size, the steel, a drawing and a clear total wins more jobs than a number said on the phone. It also protects you when the customer later asks for "just one more rail".
